Who Said That?

Every recorded board meeting on this site comes with a transcript where each line carries a name. Getting the words right turned out to be the easy part. Getting the names right is the work, and this page is the honest account of how we do it, what went wrong, and how we now grade ourselves.

Behind this text: one glowing cloud per voice in the room. Every so often two of them drift together and become one blob. Pulling them back apart, correctly, is the whole job.

📝The words: the easy part (now)

Recordings are transcribed by AssemblyAI's Universal‑3.5 Pro, the best model its pre-recorded service offers (we re-check: on November 13, 2026 it still accepted only that model and its predecessor). A speech model on its own has never heard of Boulan Park or Kristine Griffor, so before it listens to a meeting it is handed up to 1,000 key terms: a curated roster of trustees, administrators, schools and acronyms, plus every proper noun mined from that meeting's own board packet: the vendors on the bid tab, the presenters, the students named in a resolution.

The November 10, 2026 workshop, start to finish:

1.56 GB→the Teams recording (3 hours, 14 minutes of room camera)
93 MB→the audio track
32,272→words, in 875 lines (more than 350 new key terms came from its packet)
12→anonymous voice clusters ("Speaker A" through "Speaker L")
40→agenda chapters that seek the video

Cost of the transcription itself: about ninety cents. The expensive part is everything after.

🗣️The names: the hard part

The speech model does not know who anyone is. It hears voices, groups them into clusters, and labels them A, B, C. Turning "Speaker F" into a person happens afterward, and every step of that can fail quietly. Three failure modes cover nearly everything we have found:

  • Two voices, one cluster. Two trustees who sit apart but sound alike were merged into a single "speaker" at three consecutive meetings (September 1, October 6, November 10).
  • One voice, two clusters. A chair who speaks for 85 minutes can come back as two or three "people."
  • The right cluster, the wrong name. The automatic naming step is a text model: it reads the transcript and guesses who is talking from context. It once gave the Director of Athletics' entire presentation, about 8,000 words, to a trustee who happens to share his first name.

A transcript that says "Unidentified speaker" is honest. A transcript that puts a trustee's name on someone else's words is worse than no name at all, and it reads just as confidently.

🧩The evidence stack

No single signal is trustworthy, so a name has to survive several that fail in different ways:

📜 ContentWho answers when the chair says "Stephanie?" The trustee who reads a resolution is the one who moves it. A presenter talks about "our principals"; a trustee talks about "my middle schooler."
🎥 The cameraThe board room's speaker-tracking camera cuts to whoever is talking, and the name plates are legible in the close-ups. Remote trustees' Teams tiles show who is muted.
🔊 The voice libraryEvery line gets a 192-number voiceprint (an ECAPA-TDNN speaker model). Each person's reference voice is built from meetings we have already settled by hand.
📋 The minutesThe roll call says who was in the room, so the audit can flag words put in the mouth of someone who was absent.

At the November 10 workshop each signal caught something another missed. Content and the automatic namer agreed one cluster was a trustee, Matt; eleven camera close-ups showed it was the trustee sitting next to him. Then the camera and that meeting's own voices both missed that another cluster held two trustees. The voice library caught it: one woman's lines matched her voice from earlier meetings at 0.84 to 0.98, and the other trustee's at 0.48.

Re-running the same check does not make it more right. Adding a check that fails differently does.

⚖️The confidence rule

A line gets a name only when the voice and the context agree. When they conflict and a video frame does not settle it, the line is published as "Unidentified speaker," and a quote that is not settled stays out of anything we post.

For the November 10 workshop that meant scoring every one of the 875 lines against the voice library: 478 confirmed, 17 moved to the person whose voice they clearly were, and the rest checked by context. In the end 2.4% of the words (mostly "okay," "thank you" and "sure") are unidentified. That number is not a failure. It is the part we refuse to guess.

📏Grading ourselves: the back-test

A method you cannot measure is a method you cannot trust. So we hid the answers: for each of four meetings settled line by line by hand, reference voices were built from the other three, every line was scored blind, and the result was compared with the human answer key (2,449 lines in all).

99.3%of lines it names are named correctly (score ≥ 0.60)
64%of lines it is confident enough to name
1.4%of guest lines wrongly given a library voice
2real errors it found in an already-published transcript, now fixed

Lower the bar to 0.50 and it names more lines (72%) but starts mistaking guests, people with no reference voice like a visiting presenter, for board members almost six percent of the time. That trade-off is why the bar sits where it does, and why it is adjusted per person: each regular's own lines score between 0.69 and 0.82 against their reference, so a "match" far below someone's usual score is treated as a stranger. (One reference turned out to be unusable, its own owner scoring 0.30 against it, and it is excluded entirely.)

The one false alarm was itself instructive: a voice that "matched" a trustee who, the video showed, was a muted Teams tile at the time. A reference built from six lines is not a reference.

🗄️What it found in the archive corrected

Then we pointed the same method at the 32 meetings transcribed between January 2025 and August 2026, most of them named by the automatic step and spot-audited at the time. It turned out the automatic namer had often put the wrong name on a whole cluster: a board president and vice president swapped at an organizational meeting; recognitions presenters labeled as board members; outside trainers labeled as trustees; a cabinet member's voice under a trustee's name.

591,604→words in those meetings
139,919→words whose speaker was corrected
34,401→words that became "Unidentified speaker" (a wrong name removed, no right one provable)
5→meetings that came back clean

Every proposed change is held to the same rule: a cluster is renamed only when a clear majority of its lines match one person's voice above that person's and that meeting's bar. Ten renames fell short and were held back, and we checked the held ones on video and by content. Three would have turned a visiting presenter into a trustee, and the bar was right to stop them. Three others were real errors the bar was simply too cautious to fix (among them the "trustee" whose cluster said "athletics" 68 times), and those were confirmed by hand, with the evidence written down. One could not be settled either way and stays unidentified.

Published November 13, 2026: the corrected transcripts are live on every meeting page and in the downloadable files, and the YouTube caption tracks follow over two days (YouTube rations caption uploads). Every meeting's previous version is kept, and every change is recorded with its evidence in the repository.

🔭Where this goes next

  • A library that grows. Every meeting settled by hand becomes another reference for every voice in it, and the back-test re-runs after each one, so the method keeps checking its own past work.
  • Second opinions. A local transcription (Whisper) diffed against AssemblyAI to flag names and numbers where the two disagree, and a second speaker-separation model (pyannote) to flag lines where the two split the voices differently. AssemblyAI stays the source; disagreement just tells us where to look.
  • Further back. The same check for the 2024 recordings.

All of it is in the open: the transcription/ folder holds the pipeline, the back-test (backtest_voice.py), the cluster check and the fix proposals, and every meeting's speaker evidence, with the method written up in docs/TRANSCRIPTION.md.

 Sex Education Advisory Boards 
Commonly Asked Questions 
What is the legal requirement for a sex education advisory board in Michigan school 
districts? 
Mandate for an advisory board.  A school district shall not offer this [sex education] instruction 
unless a sex education advisory board is established by the board of the school district. 
§380.1507(5) 
Composition of advisory board.  The board of a school district shall determine terms of service 
for the sex education advisory board, the number of members to serve on the advisory board, 
and a membership selection process that reasonably reflects the school district population, and 
shall appoint 2 co-chairs for the advisory board, at least 1 of whom is a parent of a child 
attending a school operated by the school district. At least 1/2 of the members of the sex 
education advisory board shall be parents who have a child attending a school operated by the 
school district, and a majority of these parent members shall be individuals who are not 
employed by a school district. The board of a school district shall include pupils of the school 
district, educators, local clergy, and community health professionals on the sex education 
advisory board.  §380.1507(5) 
Notice of meetings.  Written or electronic notice of a sex education advisory board meeting 
shall be sent to each member at least 2 weeks before the date of the meeting. §380.1507(6) 
Role of advisory board.  The advisory board shall do all of the following:  
 Establish program goals and objectives for pupil knowledge and skills that are likely to 
reduce the rates of sex, pregnancy, and sexually transmitted diseases. This subdivision 
does not prohibit a school district from establishing additional program goals and objectives 
that are not contrary to this section, section 1169, or section 1507b.  
 Review the materials and methods of instruction used and make recommendations to the 
board of the school district for implementation. The advisory board shall take into 
consideration the school district's needs, demographics, and trends, including, but not 
limited to, teenage pregnancy rates, sexually transmitted disease rates, and incidents of 
student sexual violence and harassment.  
 At least once every 2 years, evaluate, measure, and report the attainment of program 
goals and objectives established under subdivision (a). The board of a school district shall 
make the resulting report available to parents in the school district.  §380.1507(5a) 
What are the roles and responsibilities of advisory board members? 
In general, advisory board members are expected to: 
 Regularly attend advisory board meetings and prepare for being an effective member at 
the meetings as needed.  
 Seek accurate and current information regarding content and instructional strategies that 
will help them make informed decisions as board members and practical recommendations 
to the board of education based on best practice.  
 Be advocates and spokespersons for the board decisions and the adopted program.   
 Share ideas and concerns and work cooperatively in a way that maintains the board as a 
partnership of people who are willing to work toward healthy sexual development of youth 
and reducing sexual risk behaviors. 
 Support the public hearing process for proposed program revisions. 
 Contribute to an evaluation plan and report regarding attainment of program goals and 
objectives. 
 Respect the decisions of the board of education regarding their recommendations.

What helps the advisory board to stay focused and on task? 
 Respectful and welcoming board leadership and conduct of members.  
 An understanding of the purpose of the board and the responsibilities of the individual 
members. 
 A mission statement that has been reached by consensus and can be reviewed when not all 
agree on an issue. 
 Established board processes and procedures. 
 Established tasks, commitment to the priority of completing the tasks, and established 
deadlines. 
 Meetings that build enthusiasm for the topic and generate commitment and a feeling of 
accomplishment from the participants. 
 A process that capitalizes on the unique expertise of all members. 
 A meeting environment that allows for healthy discussions and disagreement, but also 
facilitates group decision-making based on an agreed upon approach (e.g., consensus, 
majority). 
 A meeting calendar set ahead of time to avoid conflicts with other meetings.  
 Meeting reminder notices.  The legislation requires that written or electronic meeting 
notices be sent to each advisory board member at least two weeks prior to a meeting. 
 Published agendas. 
 Timely follow-up to issues and questions raised. 
 Respect for busy schedules and commitments to families and careers/jobs of members. 
 Starting and ending meetings on time. 
 Prepared and available materials. 
 Feedback from the board of education regarding progress of the advisory board. 
What is the relationship between the sex education advisory board and the local 
school board? 
 The advisory board makes recommendations to the local school board regarding materials 
and methods used in instruction. 
 Prior to board action, the district must hold at least two public hearings on proposed 
revisions, at least a week apart, in the manner required under Section 1201 for board 
meetings.  
 The local school board adopts recommendations and sets district policy for the program of 
instruction.  §380.1507(6) 
Can the district combine health-related committees? 
Some districts consider combining the sex education advisory board with other district-level 
health-related committees such as the wellness policy committee or the coordinated school 
health team.  The district should consider pros and cons of consolidating committees.  Pros 
may include:  fewer people needed to sit on district wide committees, less district costs in 
human resources, greater communication across committees, broader understanding of 
district-wide health issues and programs, and fewer meetings for those on all committees.  
Cons may include:  too much work for one committee to accomplish, confusion about purpose 
of committee and roles of committee members, lack of committee member commitment to 
broadened focus, and members burning out from too much work.

I had a telephone conference with the Liaison Committee earlier today to discuss 
whether to pursue an appeal to the Michigan Supreme Court on the issues adversely 
decided in the Court of Appeals’ decision. The discussion concerned the following 
points. 
Initially it needs to be observed that unlike appeals to the Court of Appeals the 
decision whether to decide  issues presented in a given case from a lower court is 
entirely discretionary with t he Supreme Court. An application for this to occur is 
through a process known as “seeking leave to appeal.”  It is also important to note 
that the Supreme Court does not consider itself an “error correction” court; meaning 
that the Justices of the Court are  quite outspoken that they do not focus on 
examining and resolving legally incorrect decision from lower courts. Rather, the 
Supreme Court focuses on deciding issue of law presented from lower court 
decisions which broadly apply in Michigan, such as decidi ng the application of 
constitutional or statutory provisions which are at the core of how our state’s legal 
system functions  and is usually limited to issues of law which have statewide 
implications. As such, it would not be productive to argue in seeking leave to appeal 
on the numerous points of law decided in this September 20 , 2016 decision which 
I believe are plainly wrong and should be rectified; no matter how strongly I believe 
and can demonstrate them to be violative of the Headlee Amendment to the 
Constitution. As a final point it would be unproductive to present numerous issues 
for decision to the Supreme Court in an application for leave to appeal. Ideally, it 
would be only one or possibly two legal issues requested to be considered. That 
requires that we be selective on which of several issues decided by the Court of 
Appeals which we may now seek to appeal to the Supreme Court. 
With these points in mind in mind, during my discussion with the Liaison 
Committee earlier today, I recommended that if w e are to attempt to pursue an 
appeal further it should be limited to one issue of statewide significance in an 
application for leave to appeal to the Supreme Court. The issue of the greatest 
significance financially to local units of governments, foremost including school 
districts, is the demonstrated practice of the Legislature when required to fund the

This practice began for school districts following the decision reached in our 
favor in 1997 establishing t hat special education services were mandated by state 
law within the meaning of the Headlee Amendment and weren’t being fully funded.  
Therefore, the State was required by operation of that decision to fund the costs of 
those services under the Amendment. Up to the point of that decision, the Attorney 
General’s office argued on behalf of state government that special education 
services were not mandated by state law and, accordingly, state government had no 
duty under the Headlee Amendment to fund those ser vices. When faced with the 
reality that they had to fund these services by operation of the Supreme Court’s 
1997 decision the Legislature adopted legislation which shifted the costs of 
whatever additional money was required to be paid to reimburse those co sts by a 
dollar for dollar deduction from each school’s general operating fund allocation as 
the resource to make that payment.  
We filed suit to contest that funding scheme to the Michigan Court of Appeals 
and prevailed. The State elected not to appeal to the Supreme Court but, unbowed, 
the Legislature at the Governor’s behest devised a new funding scheme in what 
came to be known as the three bucket funding scheme. This scheme accomplishes 
the same result but in a more complex manner than the Legislature’s previous more 
obvious scheme. That was also challenged by our group of school districts to the 
Court of Appeals which refused to rule that this scheme was contrary to the Headlee 
Amendment. We subsequently sought leave to appeal to the Michigan Supreme 
Court but the Court declined in its discretion to grant leave to appeal on that issue. 
It is important to note , that a decision by the Court not to grant leave to appeal is 
not even by implication a decision on the merits of the decision of the lower court 
that was the subject of the application. This is well established law in Michigan. 
In a similar manner to the special education non -funding scheme described 
above, the Legislature in the wake of our successful argument to the Supreme Court 
in a 2010 decis ion establishing that the Center for Educational Performance and 
Information (CEPI) is imposing mandates on schools statewide within the meaning 
of the Headlee Amendment which must be funded  and adopted a categorical 
funding appropriation under the School Aid Act, §152a, in 2011. This was to 
ostensibly meet its Headlee funding responsibility but then concurrently provided 
that each school district ’s general operating revenues will be reduced in an equal 
amount to the amount of funding they receive under §152a. That funding scheme 
exists through the present time. 
In other words, the State totally avoids any responsibility to actually fund this 
Constitutional responsibility. The Headlee Amendment expresses this 
responsibility as plainly as the following words “The state is prohibited from

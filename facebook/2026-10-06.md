*Troy School Board — Workshop Meeting, October 6, 2026*

Meeting Recording - https://youtu.be/7JyXa8h7WrA

**Most of this two hour, 50 minute workshop went to the district's annual student achievement presentation, with trustees pressing on elementary reading, special education and why math recovery lags reading. The rest of the night covered the district's new director of safety and security, four purchases headed to the October 20 meeting (ovens for Baker Middle School, bond furniture, an emergency chiller for Morse Elementary and a Boulan Park re-bid), the administration's three-year Digital Delusion work plan, which most trustees said is too slow, and a $70,000 facilities study the board president would rather postpone. Emina Alic and Matt Haupt attended by Microsoft Teams.**

**Safety and security**

Paul Crane, the district's new director of safety and security, introduced himself: 20 years with the Saginaw police, 14 of them as a command officer up to deputy chief, then 10 years as Kettering University's director of safety and emergency preparedness. The state-required standard response protocol has been rolled out, with go-bag and reunification-site details and a QR code on internal postings (not public documents). Building evacuation maps are being redrawn room by room from each school's routes and digitized. Five of the required drills now happen district-wide on the same day — an evacuation drill ran September 22 — with feedback gathered through the Safety and Security Committee. Asked by Vital Anne what stood out to him as a newcomer, Crane named the door-swipe access and camera coverage, and said he and IT are looking at cameras for the new playgrounds. "As a former athlete, I do believe you play like you practice."

**Purchases headed to the October 20 meeting**

· Baker Middle School ovens: two stackable Unox ovens from Stafford-Smith for $49,463, from the Food Service Fund, against a $49,000 budget. Of three cooperative bids, one was incomplete and one depended on a manufacturer discount that did not hold up. Anne asked whether a single qualified bid needs a rebid; Dan Trudel said no, since cooperative pricing is largely bid in advance. The purchase is part of the state-required spend-down of excess food service fund balance; after the Troy High project Trudel expects little or no excess this year.
· School meals: Alic asked the board not to lose track of meal participation, discussed in May. Anne recalled a rate of 60 to 65 percent: "Considering that we get free meals, it should be far higher than that." Monthly numbers will go into the food service newsletters, with a fuller look around January.
· Bond furniture: the bond replaces the elementary schools' original built-in classroom casework. Martell Elementary will pilot mobile and wall-mounted storage in its classrooms — a lengthy pilot, rotated through buildings so teachers get hands-on time before a decision for the other 11 elementaries; Costello gets a one-off storage addition in its media center; and conference rooms at the Services Building get new tables. TMP's recommendation letters set not-to-exceed amounts of $120,000 for Costello and $30,000 for Martell; the Services Building package is $48,942.88.
· Morse Elementary chiller: the building's central chiller failed late last spring, and the school has run on a rented generator and temporary cooling since. Replacement is just over $200,000 from the bond, through the OMNIA cooperative; with a 10 to 12 week lead time, the goal is installation in February, before cooling season. It had been planned about two years out.
· Boulan Park acoustic wall panels: the low bidder withdrew after missing part of the scope; the next bid is about $60,000 higher and goes on the October consent agenda.

**Student achievement**

Christine DiPilato and Kristine Griffor presented, crediting the district's data team. Three measures: M-STEP (each spring, against state standards), NWEA MAP (math, three times a year, new to elementary this fall) and classroom benchmarks. The comparison group is Oakland County's top five districts, which administration stressed are not peer districts.

· Elementary: math proficiency in grades 3 to 5 is 70.3 percent, above pre-COVID levels. Reading is trending down at every grade, and Griffor named it plainly: "While we are above, we know that this is an area that we really need to improve on." Of 123 first graders below benchmark in 2022-23, 25 are still below in fifth grade. This fall's NWEA puts the typical Troy third-to-fifth grader around the 70th percentile nationally.
· Middle school: reading rose from 69.8 to 71.1 percent over five years and math from 64.2 to 68.1. Of 221 students below the reading benchmark on the fifth-grade M-STEP, 141 were above it on the eighth-grade PSAT; in math, 85 of 294. Supplanted (pull-out) reading classes are gone, and special education resource rooms have become integrated studies classes built around students' IEP goals.
· High school: 79.5 percent met the SAT reading benchmark, up from 75.3 and the best of the comparison districts, with steady gains at Athens over three years; 65.7 percent met the math benchmark. Every high school math class is now taught by a certified math teacher, with no supplanted classes. AP students took 4,340 exams and 86.1 percent scored 3 or higher; 106 of 124 International Academy East candidates earned the IB diploma; and 22.1 percent of English learners tested out of the program, against about 14 percent countywide.
· Chronic absenteeism was not presented: the report the district sent the state had errors (a divisor problem), and administration did not want to show numbers it could not stand behind.

From the trustees:

· Audra Melton asked for a better way to show growth for special education students — "there are gains made that don't show up here" — and for training for teachers of the new integrated studies classes.
· Stephanie Zendler asked for NWEA results by subgroup, how students with reading IEPs get specialized instruction without pull-out classes, and why math recovery lags reading. And, again, dual enrollment: "I'm going to keep hounding this."
· Anne asked whether staffing cuts will make special education results worse. Griffor: "That's not our plan." On why Troy High and Athens differ, DiPilato pointed to the middle schools that feed them: "where they start on day 1 is not the same square." Melton added that the courses and expectations are the same at both schools.
· Alic asked whether students' limited access to their own tests and quizzes holds back math growth between eighth and eleventh grade.

**The Digital Delusion: three years is too long**

Following the board's summer read, administration proposed a three-year plan: year one for surveys, family sessions, a draft district technology philosophy and a cost audit; year two to adopt it and pilot classroom guidelines; year three to expand, with a parent advisory committee and an annual transparency report. Most trustees wanted it faster.

· Alic: "3 years seem like a really long time." She wanted data gathered this year, changes from next fall, and the cost of all the district's technology at this meeting.
> "I'm not inclined to approve another million-dollar purchase of iPads, you know, 7 months from now." — Emina Alic
· Melton: a concrete elementary plan by next fall, middle and high school the year after; from her school visits, high schoolers would rather have a Chromebook or laptop than an iPad.
· Zendler: an accounting of "every penny we spend on tech" before next year's budget work, then policy, with teacher input gathered through the TEA rather than a generic survey. She said she was agnostic about AI in the spring until she saw it affect her middle schooler. "I think we can do it in a much more rapid pace if our goal is to do less."
· Haupt agreed year one could happen this year, but cautioned against acting before hearing from the community:
> "I would rather do it the right way and have everybody on board and make sure we're all moving in the same direction." — Matt Haupt
· Alic answered that the Anxious Generation cell-phone review gathered parent, student and staff feedback in a couple of months, and is the blueprint.
· Anne: a software audit is "realistically easy to do, maybe within a month," the board drafts a technology policy, and families help write guidelines by age. "We feel a sense of urgency."

DiPilato summed up: with some tweaks, the year-one steps match what the board asked for. She will separate policy from philosophy, tie the work to the strategic plan, and build in ways for staff to give feedback; a "pilot" may simply mean the first year of rollout.

**A $70,000 facilities study**

Anne questioned a proposed Plante Moran study of how every building is used — "my bigger concern is actually the sticker shock" — and suggested waiting a year or two now that the new middle school is open. Trudel said it extends the $4,000 to $5,000 enrollment study done each year. Enrollment is around 12,000 and expected to fall by 100 to 150 a year for four or five years. School of choice is not filling its 100 seats (about 65 two years ago, in the 80s this year), partly because those students are steered to under-enrolled buildings on the east side. Any conversation about repurposing a building or redistricting takes two to three years. Trudel offered to have Plante Moran present at the November workshop, and said capital maintenance money set aside before the bond could pay for it.

**Board communication**

Zendler asked that when one trustee emails a question, the answer goes to all of them. Alic said reply-all was standard practice when she and Haupt joined the board and stopped about a year and a half ago, and that one answer she received never made it into the board communiqué. Anne said answers are usually summarized there. DiPilato: "I'll make sure to loop Rich into that."

**One important caveat**

*This was a workshop, so nothing was voted on; the ovens, furniture, chiller and Boulan re-bid all go to the October 20 regular meeting. The achievement figures are the district's own slides, the absenteeism data was held back pending corrections, and the furniture amounts are TMP's not-to-exceed recommendations, not final prices.*

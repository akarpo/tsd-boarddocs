*Troy School Board: Regular Meeting, October 20, 2026*

Meeting Recording - https://youtu.be/f5taR4cWg8g

**Most of this one hour, 50 minute regular meeting went to the district's annual student achievement presentation, a shorter public version of what trustees heard at the October 6 workshop. The board also approved all four purchases from that workshop (ovens for Baker Middle School, furniture for Martell, Costello and the Services Building, and a new chiller for Morse Elementary), recognized 73 National Merit semifinalists and Schroeder Elementary's social and emotional learning work, and passed resolutions for Learning Disabilities Awareness Month and Principal Appreciation Month. Six of seven trustees attended; Nancy Philippart was absent.**

**Recognitions**

· Schroeder Elementary: Principal Lindsay Keegan and her students showed the board their cozy corners, a sensory hallway and "hot cocoa breathing," part of two years of work with a behavior specialist across all 12 elementary schools. Nicholas, a fourth grader who asked the board last year to renovate Schroeder's bathrooms, reported they are done.
· Learning Disabilities Awareness Month: read by Audra Melton, passed 6-0.
· Principal Appreciation Month: read by Stephanie Zendler, passed.
· National Merit: 73 Troy seniors were named 2027 semifinalists, out of about 16,000 nationwide. Vital Anne read every name. The district will honor them at a reception Monday, October 26, at 6 p.m. at Troy High.

**Student achievement: math up, reading down in elementary**

Kristine Griffor presented elementary and Christine DiPilato middle and high school, comparing Troy with Oakland County's five historically top districts, which they again stressed are not peer districts. The figures below are from the district's slides for 2025-26 testing.

· Elementary math: 70.3 percent of third to fifth graders proficient, against 48.3 percent countywide, second in the county behind Novi (73.1), and above pre-pandemic levels. On NWEA this fall, Troy's third to fifth graders sat between the 69th and 74th percentiles nationally.
· Elementary reading: 66.1 percent proficient, against 50.1 countywide (Birmingham led at 70.1), and falling in every grade over five years, about a point a year in third grade and two in fourth. Griffor tied the response to the state's new literacy law (screening, Tier 1 materials, professional learning, curricular resources, MTSS and English learners) and to the phonics pilot trustees visited at Martell. Of 123 students below benchmark in first grade, 25 still were in fifth.
· Middle school: 71.1 percent proficient in reading (M-STEP and PSAT 8) and 68.1 in math, both trending up. DiPilato credited the new math curriculum, now in its third year, and the end of supplanted classes, where students with disabilities were taught in a separate course instead of the core class. Special education resource rooms have become integrated studies classes grouped by IEP goal area. Two middle school reading programs are being piloted, and the accelerated math pathway continues into seventh and eighth grade.
· High school: 79.5 percent met the SAT reading benchmark and 65.7 percent math, against 60.5 and 37.0 countywide. Grade 11 science proficiency rose to 60.2 percent from 36.4 in 2022-23, which DiPilato tied to the new K-8 science curriculum and the graduation requirement that every student take chemistry, biology, physics and environmental science.
· Special education at the high school, in DiPilato's words, in this first year of placing every student in core classes:

> "I wouldn't say that anything that we're doing here is making a significant difference in that outcome." (Christine DiPilato)

· AP and IB: 2,024 students, 55.1 percent of high schoolers, are in AP courses this year, the most ever. Of individual AP students last year, 89.7 percent scored a 3 or higher on an exam. At International Academy East, 106 of 124 candidates (85.5 percent) earned the IB diploma.
· Also: 130 students are dual enrolled this year, and 22.1 percent of English learners tested out of the program, against 14.1 percent countywide and 8.8 percent statewide.
· Chronic absenteeism: 13 percent of students missed more than 10 percent of school. The district rebuilt the figure itself after the data it reported to the state proved wrong. DiPilato: "It's a pretty flat line for us in Troy. It's not necessarily an acceptable line."

From the trustees:

· Anne asked whether the current sixth graders lag as a group, and wondered whether COVID, which hit when they were in kindergarten and first grade, played a part. Griffor said the team looks at them school by school and student by student.
· Anne asked whether cutting special education staff will hurt results: "And you think we won't have any much impact on our student outcomes because of that?" DiPilato said secondary special education is staffed to the ratio the district was given and that the changes are meant to close the gap. "I know hope is not a strategy, but the strategy is we're moving more students into that Tier One section."
· Zendler thanked administration for adding the dual enrollment and absenteeism slides. Emina Alic asked whether absences concentrate at one level; DiPilato said historically they peak in kindergarten and 12th grade and offered a breakdown.

**Purchases approved**

All four came from the October 6 workshop, and Dan Trudel said nothing had changed since.

· Baker Middle School ovens: two Unox ovens from Stafford-Smith for $49,463, from the Food Service Fund.
· Martell and Costello furniture: not to exceed $150,000 from the 2026 bond, through consortium pricing. The architect's letter splits it $120,000 for Costello's media center shelving and small-group areas and $30,000 for a classroom storage pilot at Martell, the start of replacing the elementary schools' original built-in cabinets. Anne noted the board usually sees exact amounts; Trudel said the pilot needs some flexibility as pieces are finalized. Passed 6-0.
· Services Building furniture: $48,942.88 from the 2026 bond, for conference rooms and the teaching and learning workroom. Passed 6-0.
· Morse Elementary chiller: $210,309.30 to ThermalNetics, from the Capital Projects and Sinking Funds. The building's chiller failed last spring and the school has run on temporary cooling since. Trudel said approving both funds gives the district flexibility to make the most of each. Passed 6-0.

**Board comments: the science of reading, with urgency**

· Zendler asked to share what trustees learned at the MASB conference at the November workshop, and pointed to new state guidance on the timing of science of reading training for staff: "I just want to sort of continue to stress a sense of urgency and careful planning so that we are at the front end versus the back end."
· Melton praised the growth in the data and the phonics lesson at Martell, then turned to intervention. She noted the district still uses Reading Recovery, though its consultant, NSK12's Levinson, recommended a program that serves more students at lower cost, and cited a decline of about 87 percent in its use nationally. "I think we need to look at products that we're using that are able to service the most students at the lowest cost." She also questioned the cost and staff time of the Fountas and Pinnell reading benchmark.
· Ayesha Potts, who stayed an extra night at the MASB conference for the advanced finance session, was impressed by the finished Smith Middle School: "Nothing wrong with saying that we need to improve in some places."
· Matt Haupt promoted the Athens Theater Company's "Almost, Maine," invited to the Michigan Thespian Festival on December 12, with public shows November 6 to 8.
· Alic reminded voters that the school board race is on the back of the ballot, with early voting October 24 to November 1 at the Troy Community Center.
· Anne thanked the staff behind Smith Middle School, congratulated the Troy High tennis team on its state championship, and asked about E-Rate. Dr. Richard Machesky said the federal program supports much of the district's fiber and technology infrastructure, is considered for cuts most years, and that its loss would fall on the general fund.

**Also at this meeting**

· Public comment on agenda items: I spoke, asking the board for three things: protect the district's financial health (fund balance expected to close last year just above 15 percent, with a $12 million budget reduction still required this year, against 19.5 percent expected at Rochester), finish restoring tracked courses (Math 7-8 in 2027-28, and a process and timeline for tracked English 9 and 10, commonly referred to as Honors), and open up the district's curriculum so every parent can see the skills taught in every subject and grade.
· Consent agenda, passed 6-0: September 1 and 22 minutes, the August 2026 Treasurer's Report, and a $61,696 net increase for Boulan Park's acoustic wall panels after the low bidder withdrew, moving the work to Clark Contracting Services.
· Student reports from International Academy East (Ahyoung Song), Troy College and Career High School (Shyanne Kurzynski) and Troy High; Athens had no representative.
· Coming up: a community conversation Thursday, October 29, at 9:30 at the central office; no school Friday, October 30 (professional development); a strategic planning retreat Saturday, November 14; the next workshop November 10 at 6 p.m. in the Administration Building and the next regular meeting November 17 at 7 p.m.
· Adjourned at 8:55 p.m.

**One important caveat**

*The achievement figures are the district's own slides and presenters, for last spring's testing, and the comparison group is the county's five historically top districts, not the state's peer districts. The absenteeism figure is the district's own recount, since the numbers it sent the state were wrong. The $150,000 elementary furniture approval is a ceiling, not a price; final amounts come from consortium contracts.*

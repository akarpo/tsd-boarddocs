*Troy School Board: Workshop Meeting, November 10, 2026*

Meeting Recording - https://youtu.be/gQwqU5icsIo

**This three hour, 14 minute workshop opened with the 2025-26 audit, which has the General Fund ending the year just above the Board's 15 percent goal, and spent its longest stretch on a special education update: more students moving in with IEPs, programs near capacity, and a high school structure still adjusting to the end of supplanted classes. The rest of the night covered a proposed Plante Moran study of how every building is used, an early structural steel award for the Athens addition, the first four leased fleet trucks, the Digital Delusion work plan, returning tests to students, a new handbook for modified grades, a recommendation to pause schools of choice for 2027-28, and two items from the Superintendent: a do-over of the Troy High JV baseball field and the deputy superintendent proposal.**

**The audit: the General Fund lands above 15 percent**

Dan Trudel presented with Kyle Anderson, the district's new Finance Director, who took over the audit mid-stream at the start of the summer. Plante & Moran will give the formal presentation at next week's regular meeting. The results: an unmodified (clean) opinion, and no findings in the federal single audit (this year's tested program was child nutrition), in bond and sinking fund compliance, or under the Budgeting Act.

· General Fund: revenue of $199,809,780 against spending of $202,195,371, leaving a fund balance of $31,328,204, or 15.49 percent of expenditures on the auditor's slide. The original and amended budgets had it dipping below 14 percent. Trudel credited positions left unfilled mid-year (often covered by retired teachers as long-term substitutes) and careful building budgets: "So kudos to everybody around this organization."
· The year's deficit came in at $2,385,591 against $5,488,478 in the final budget. Dr. Machesky said that leaves this year's reductions under "far less pressure," and that the district is close to structural balance: "Couple million off on a $100 to $200 million budget is pretty darn good."
· Adding back: over $1 million in support restored so far this year, including elementary secretaries, teacher consultant positions and student support aides. Eight aides have been added since school started, for students who need help but have no IEP; almost 160 aide positions were part of the $12 million in cuts. The first budget amendment is targeted for January.
· Direct instruction is 64.72 percent of General Fund spending, against an Oakland County average of 58.27 percent, and Trudel said the goal is to find further reductions elsewhere.
· Enrollment and funding: the fall count is running slightly above the 11,869 students budgeted, and with the state's inflation adjustment the foundation allowance is $11,086 per student. Troy sits in the middle of Oakland County districts on per-pupil foundation revenue, and the statewide average fund balance is over 24 percent.
· Bond interest: the first bond series has earned close to $30 million in interest, of which $8 to $9 million will go to the IRS in about two years under the arbitrage rules for tax-exempt bonds. The second series, sold this spring, is invested to stay under that limit.

**A study of how every building is used**

Keith McDonald of Plante Moran Realpoint, a former deputy and interim superintendent at Bloomfield Hills, presented a building utilization study. Plante Moran has done Troy's enrollment projections for more than a decade; this would add school-by-school projections, a room-by-room classification of every building, functional and contractual capacity, and operating costs per building, in about two months, with McDonald walking each building with its principal. He said the district lost almost 1,100 students from 2017-18 to last year, about 8.2 percent, and the firm's last projection shows a loss of about 2,000 between 2019 and 2030. An underused building costs about $10 a square foot a year.

Trudel tied it to the schools of choice decision, the three new middle schools and the special education restructuring: "This is a multi-year planning strategy." Vital Anne questioned the timing:

*"Shouldn't we be doing this study and review before we build a brand new building so that we know what we should be building?" (Vital Anne)*

Trudel said the 2022 Facilities Review Committee looked at building condition and was very high level on enrollment, and that the decline is now reaching the elementary schools. Machesky stressed that a utilization study is not a closing list: "It's not just about potentially closing buildings." Repurposing space for special education programs, adult transition or early childhood (six buildings now host a satellite GSRP program) are all options. Stephanie Zendler asked for a staff communication plan "so we don't create, like, chaos," and said a session at the MASB conference showed a district using the same kind of study to plan attendance areas, grade configurations and busing. Anne asked how special education programs are counted; McDonald said existing programs are kept at their current size. The proposal goes on the next regular meeting agenda. No cost was discussed.

**Athens addition: structural steel ordered early**

· Structural steel for the Athens High School addition goes to Commercial Contracting Corporation, the low of six bids, at $7,951,352 plus an 8 percent contingency, $8,587,460.16 in all. It is ahead of the other trades because of material lead times ("We'll blame it on the data centers"). It is the first large purchase from the 2026 Capital Projects Fund.
· Trudel said the Athens bids overall are coming in $8 to $10 million under a budget of nearly $100 million. The remaining Athens bids come to the December 1 workshop. The addition takes the first two years; the rest of the building follows once students are in it.

**Leasing the first four fleet trucks**

Under the Enterprise agreement the Board approved a couple of months ago, the district will lease about 18 vehicles over four years, starting with some of its oldest, instead of buying replacements, which Trudel called "about a break-even scenario" with a more reliable fleet. The first four are two Ford F-350s and two F-550s. The 60-month quotes are $1,212.40 a month for the F-350 with a plow and $1,783.95 a month for the F-550 dump truck with a plow and spreader, paid from the General Fund. Anne asked whether each set of vehicles needs Board approval; Trudel said he brings them "for transparency purposes."

**Special education: more move-ins, fuller programs**

Sarah Smotherman, Director of Special Education, presented with supervisors Jamie Evans, Erica Conklin and Hillary Pulliam.

· The district serves more than 1,400 students with IEPs, 11.7 percent of enrollment. For the first time, autism eligibility exceeds specific learning disability.
· Following the county's revised plan, the district now has five Level 2 classrooms (it had none) and eight Level 3 programs (it had three), moving students into less restrictive settings.
· Move-ins: 96 students have transferred in with an IEP this year, against 70 at this point last year, and 27 of them needed a categorical program. Of 100 schools of choice students with IEPs, 26 are in categorical programs. Machesky said that of the 82 schools of choice kindergartners and first graders admitted, 17 needed services.
· Capacity: many Level 3 and Level 4 programs are full or close to it. Describing a resident move-in likely to need a Level 4 seat, Smotherman said her response was: "where do we have space?" Machesky said a new program means a teacher, two paras and possibly aides: "Significant cost to start a program."
· Early childhood: Early On serves 56 families, and new state and county guidance has the district serving preschoolers inside the 17 community programs that run a GSRP, with its own staff and no added funding. "It's an unfunded mandate," Anne said. Zendler: "Funded or not, Child Find is a legal obligation."
· Behavior supports: elementary schools are grouped into pods, each with an assigned behavior analyst, plus whole-class coaching. Tier 3 behavior cases have grown from 11 in fall 2023 to 35 this fall.
· Speech and language is over caseload; a new hire starts in January.

Zendler asked how the restructuring will be judged a success. Christine DiPilato said the changes are landing on top of the budget cuts, with reduced literacy and math specialist support and high special education caseloads, and that the current structure is "not sustainable for anyone who's in the organization right now." Zendler said she was asking as an advocate, not a critic: "we cut the deepest in our budget cuts in special ed. Like 35% of our cuts came from special ed, our most vulnerable population." Anne asked for objective measures of how special education students are doing; Smotherman pointed to NWEA growth.

**Digital Delusion: a district philosophy by spring, rollout in fall 2027**

DiPilato laid out this year's plan: an audit of software, hardware, subscriptions, staffing and their cost by winter break; a district philosophy on technology through the tech committee and principals, to the Board around January; a survey of students, families, staff and community in February and March; adoption in spring 2027; and rollout in fall 2027. The philosophy would cover screen time by age, when technology should and should not be used, grading and feedback, adult versus student use, and how the existing acceptable use and AI policies fit.

Emina Alic widened the frame to student data and to AI in the classroom. On a closed AI system used in grades 3 to 5:

*"the danger isn't whether the system is closed or unclosed. It's the fact that the system is replacing the thought process that the 3rd grader needs to have" (Emina Alic)*

She raised the online-only GoFan athletic passes: "So GoFan knows when a particular child attended a particular game." Anne asked for a process to approve new technology and to re-evaluate it every year or two; Trudel said the technology director has built a request workflow.

**Getting tests back**

DiPilato said teaching and learning shares the goal of meaningful feedback, and described error analysis sheets and larger question banks, with test security as the teachers' concern. "We got our tests back," Anne said. Alic said test security "sounds like Silicon Valley tech language to me," noted that Bloomfield Hills returns an assessment when a parent asks, and said math in particular builds on itself. DiPilato called it "work that we need to be able to do with our teachers, not to our teachers." Alic asked that next spring's student survey not be a single in-school day and that it include parents; Zendler added teachers.

**Modified grades are back**

DiPilato walked through the new accommodations and modifications handbook. An accommodation changes how a student reaches the material; a modification changes the standard itself, is decided by the IEP team with the family, and shows on the report card and transcript with an asterisk ("a modified B"). She expects it to apply to about 1 to 2 percent of students, mostly at the high schools, and said it returns a practice largely dropped after 2011, now that the state has ended supplanted classes. At Troy High, students with IEPs placed in Algebra 1 were struggling, so a co-taught foundational math course was created this fall; Athens had placed similar students in intensified algebra. DiPilato said the two schools enrolled differently: "I will take the blame for what that looks like."

**Schools of choice: pause for 2027-28**

Machesky recommended no new schools of choice seats for 2027-28, with a survey of current families about siblings, pending the building study. Trudel added that the state's enrollment stabilization funding now averages three years, about $1 million the district did not budget. No trustee objected.

**From the Superintendent**

· Troy High JV baseball field: after months of back-and-forth, testing found the field was not built to its design, and it drains clay onto the patio area. It will be regraded to drain toward the outfield, which needs a retaining wall of about 60 feet that was not in the original design. Trudel said the district's share for that wall may be under $200,000, with the contractor's liability to be sorted out. The project comes to the Board at the December 1 workshop, with work over the winter.
· Deputy superintendent: Machesky addressed the consent item pulled from the last meeting, saying misinformation had been shared publicly. He said a deputy title reflects added responsibilities, in this case the bond, that the district has had deputies twice during his tenure, and that the recommendation was meant to be cost neutral. He said the conversation will continue at a later date.

**Also at this meeting**

· No public comment.
· MASB conference reports from Zendler (the facilities study session; inclusion trainings for paras and teachers) and Anne (sessions on career and technical education, school law, start times and student advisory boards). DiPilato said this year's Superintendent's student advisory board is leaning into a year of service, pairing service projects with advisory meetings.
· Alic asked that the Board meet with the area's new state representative, state senator and congressman, with a one-page list of funding issues such as unfunded special education services.
· Anne thanked Walt Cook for running and welcomed Mary Gunn, elected to the Board on November 3, and Machesky said Gunn plans to attend Saturday's strategic planning retreat. Anne was re-elected: "You're stuck with me for another 6 years."
· The recording ends at about 9:12 p.m.

**One important caveat**

*This was a workshop, so nothing was voted on: the facilities study, the steel award and the vehicle leases come back to a regular meeting, and the rest of the Athens bids and the baseball field to the December 1 workshop. The audit figures are Plante & Moran's and the district's, the lease amounts are Enterprise's quotes, and the special education counts are the department's as of the meeting (the transfer count rose from 90 to 96 after the slides went out). This recording is the Microsoft Teams recording of the room camera, not a cable broadcast.*
